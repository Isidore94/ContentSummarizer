CORE IDEA  
The nonidentity problem illustrates that individuals might exist with different qualities of life based on parental decisions that affect their very conception, creating ethical dilemmas regarding harm and existence.

KEY LESSONS  
- TIME DEPENDENCE CLAIM: A specific sperm and egg combination leads to a unique individual; if conceived at a different time, a different individual exists, and the original does not exist at all.  
- NONIDENTITY PROBLEM: A concept from moral philosophy, it describes situations where harming a potential child does not harm the existing child because they would not exist otherwise.  
- MORAL SITUATION: A mother intentionally disabling her child to receive attention is generally viewed as wrong because it causes harm to the already conceived child.  
- DECISION CONTEXT: If a woman conceives a child while having a temporary condition that would cause disability, she may not harm the child by conceiving immediately; a different nonidentical child would exist if she waited.  
- EXISTENCE GRATITUDE: The second child, born with a disability, might feel grateful to exist, suggesting the dilemma of determining who is wronged when decisions impact existence.  
- ETHICAL DELIBERATIONS: The examples of IVF and selecting embryos demonstrate practical implications of the nonidentity problem, where parents might choose to have a disabled child.  
- TEENAGE PARENTING: Children born to teenage parents might be viewed as worse off, but their existence may still be preferable compared to never being born at all.  
- DILEMMA OF HARM: Paret argues that if a decision does not make anyone worse off, can it still be considered wrong?  
- UTILITARIANISM: One response to the nonidentity issue is the utilitarian approach that prioritizes the choice with the highest overall quality of life.  
- REPUGNANT CONCLUSION: A problem for utilitarian ethics, it states that maximizing the total number of lives may lead to a society where overall quality of life is barely satisfactory, which many find morally unacceptable.    

EVIDENCE & NUMBERS  
- In a 2007 study, 3% of IVF clinics offered services where parents intentionally chose embryos with disabilities like deafness.  
- New York's 2013 advertisements suggested that children born to teenage parents faced worse outcomes (e.g., lower graduation rates), questioning the implications of when a child is conceived.
