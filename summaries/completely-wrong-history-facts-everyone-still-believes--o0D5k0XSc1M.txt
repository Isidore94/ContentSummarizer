Completely Wrong History "Facts" Everyone Still Believes
https://youtube.com/watch?v=o0D5k0XSc1M&si=lZykhzpYRoRjqPAC

CORE IDEA
The speaker dispels common misconceptions about historical events and figures, highlighting how myths can shape our understanding. These myths often arise from cultural storytelling, artistic interpretations, and dramatic retellings rather than historical evidence. Understanding these misconceptions matters because they influence how people perceive history and cultural identities.

ARGUMENTS & LESSONS
- The popular image of ninjas wearing all black costumes originated from Japanese theater, where stagehands dressed in black and were collectively ignored by audiences. A playwright used this as a device in a play, and it was so successful that it became synonymous with ninjas in popular culture.
- Historical ninjas dressed as common people to blend in; their weapons were farm tools, making the theatrical ninja image inaccurate.
- Medieval knights could move and fight efficiently in armor, which weighed 45-55 lbs. This distribution allowed them to perform athletic feats, debunking the myth of immobility. Modern soldiers carry as much weight but in a less distributed fashion.
- Real medieval swords weighed about 3 lbs, and even large two-handed swords rarely exceeded 7 lbs. A heavy sword would slow a fighter down and result in death, which was motivation to keep them light.
- The story of the 300 Spartans omits the 7,000 Greek allies who fought at Thermopylae. The focus on Spartans overlooks the significant contributions of other groups like the Thespians and their ultimate sacrifice.
- The Wild West had stricter gun control than commonly believed. Towns like Dodge City and Tombstone required visitors to surrender weapons upon entry, contradicting the lawless image.
- Paul Revere didn't shout "The British are coming" because colonists were British subjects at the time. "The regulars are coming out" was his actual message, and his covert mission was completed by others after his capture.
- The term "Dark Ages" originated with Petrarch, an Italian poet, who thought the era following Rome's fall was bleak. However, this period sparked the foundation of universities and significant technological advancements.
- The Trojan Horse story may have been a metaphor for a siege machine or an earthquake rather than a literal event, with ancient Greek writers themselves questioning its plausibility.
- France continued using the guillotine until 1977, long past the era most associate with the device, illustrating a disconnect between perception and reality.
- Pilgrims did not wear black clothing and buckles; these were artifacts of Victorian-era paintings that aimed to depict them as stern and humble.

MENTAL MODELS & DEFINITIONS
- "Murder stroke": A historical sword-fighting technique where knights used the handle to strike heavily armored opponents.
- "Dark Ages": A term coined by Petrarch to describe the period after the fall of Rome, suggesting a decline without substantial evidence.

EVIDENCE & NUMBERS
- Researchers have put individuals in accurate replica knight armor to demonstrate knights' physical capabilities. Accounts exist of knights performing cartwheels to exhibit their agility.
- Historians analyzed murder rates in frontier towns, finding some recorded as few as one or two murders a year, with some years reporting none.
- The story of the 300 Spartans included about 7,000 allied Greek soldiers, but popular culture focuses on the Spartans due to effective ancient marketing and myth-making.

COUNTERPOINTS & LIMITS
- The story of Paul Revere omits significant contributions by other riders like Samuel Prescott, who completed the warning mission.
- The mythologizing of the 300 Spartans underplays the voluntary sacrifice of the Thespians, emphasizing how effective narrative can overshadow factual historical accounts.
- The Wild West's portrayal as lawless fails to consider the documented gun control measures and lower-than-expected violence rates in some towns.
- The notion of the "Dark Ages" as a cultural decline is based on one person's opinion, overlooking prolific advancements in education, technology, and art during the era.
