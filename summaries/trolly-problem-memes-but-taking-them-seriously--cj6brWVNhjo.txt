CORE IDEA  
Ethical dilemmas like the trolley problem challenge the distinction between consequentialism (utilitarianism) and deontology, emphasizing the importance of intentions versus outcomes in moral reasoning.

KEY LESSONS  
- Trolley problem: A moral dilemma where one must choose between sacrificing one person to save five others or allowing five people to die.  
- Intention vs. outcome: Ethics may involve not just the consequences of actions, but also the intentions behind them.  
- Utilitarianism critique: This moral framework prioritizes the outcome with the least harm, potentially overlooking the moral character of individuals based on their intentions.  
- Emotional response vs. rationality: People often react emotionally to personal moral dilemmas, leading to different decision-making processes compared to abstract ethical situations.  
- Brain in a vat: A thought experiment considering whether simulated experiences have the same moral weight as real experiences; raises questions about reality and moral responsibility.  
- Deontology: A moral framework asserting that certain actions are inherently right or wrong, irrespective of the consequences; often conflicts with consequentialist perspectives during moral dilemmas.  
- Trauma in moral choices: The psychological impact of witnessing harm varies; choosing one trauma versus five has different moral implications and emotional consequences.  
- Actionable rule: Pulling a lever to divert a train or pushing someone to save others involves different psychological burdens and moral responsibilities.

EVIDENCE & NUMBERS  
- Studies show that people are more likely to pull a lever than push a fat man, illustrating emotional responses in moral decision-making.  
- Research from Princeton in 2001 found that individuals took longer to reason through dilemmas when personal involvement was required, indicating a conflict between emotional and rational reasoning.

DO THIS  
- Reflect on your own bias in ethical dilemmas: Consider whether your decisions are being influenced more by emotion or rationality.  
- Engage in thought experiments: Explore variations of the trolley problem to understand your moral intuitions and reasoning processes.  
- Assess your decision-making criteria: Weigh the implications of your intentions versus outcomes in ethical situations you encounter in daily life.
