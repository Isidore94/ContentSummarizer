William Lane Craig Defends the Canaanite Slaughter
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WjsSHd23e0Q

CORE IDEA
The core discussion between William Lane Craig and criticisms raised by Richard Dawkins revolves around Dawkins' refusal to engage Craig in debate, justified by his objection to Craig's defense of the biblical account of the Canaanite slaughter as morally defensible. This touches on a profound question of morality and divine command theory: can God issue moral commands that would otherwise be considered immoral, and if so, how can this be reconciled with a moral understanding that condemns such acts for humans?

ARGUMENTS & LESSONS
- William Lane Craig argues that Richard Dawkins' critiques of the philosophical handling of theistic arguments, such as the ontological argument, are superficial and demonstrate Dawkins' lack of engagement with serious philosophical work.
- Craig contends that Dawkins’ argument in "The God Delusion" that God almost certainly does not exist is fallacious, as it relies on the unproven assumption that scientific explanations will eventually replace theistic ones in all cases, which does not logically entail the non-existence of God.
- The conquests of Canaan, according to Craig, were not commands of genocide but meant to drive out corrupt peoples to give the land to Israel. The command was to divest the Canaanites from the land, not necessarily to exterminate them.
- Craig defends these actions as morally permissible if commanded by God, based on divine command theory, where moral obligations are defined by God's authoritative commands rather than intrinsic moral truths.
- His defense includes the claim that God has the right to take life and, for the slain Canaanite children, provides immediate entry to heaven, presenting it as ultimately beneficial for them.
- Craig states that, while God's nature constrains His commands to be just and loving, he recognizes the difficulty in reconciling these acts with human morality, emphasizing philosophical consistency over intuitive reactions.
- He implies that Dawkins' refusal to debate is opportunistic, allowing him the moral high ground without engaging on the substantive issues.

MENTAL MODELS & DEFINITIONS
- DIVINE COMMAND THEORY: A metaethical theory that posits morality is dependent on God's commands, where moral obligations arise from imperatives issued by a qualified authority such as God.
- PHILOSOPHICAL SUPERFICIALITY: As critiqued by Craig, this refers to a lack of depth in engaging with complex theological and philosophical arguments, as he attributes to Dawkins’ critiques.

EVIDENCE & NUMBERS
- Craig cites Professor Clay Jones’ study of pre-Israelite Canaanite literature indicating severe moral corruption, supporting biblical accounts of their debauchery and justifying divine judgement.
- Dawkins’ precise criticisms are based on Craig’s defense of biblical narratives of genocide, specifically the claim that killing Canaanite children could be justified due to their heavenly afterlife—a point of intense moral repugnance for Dawkins.

COUNTERPOINTS & LIMITS
- Dawkins rejects engagement with Craig based on moral grounds, interpreting his defense of biblical narratives as immoral apologetics for genocide, which Craig claims is a convenient excuse.
- The moral loophole in divine command theory admits the possibility that God could command acts that humans find intuitively immoral, raising epistemic challenges about discerning genuine divine commands.
- Craig acknowledges the discomfort and apparent moral outrage at his conclusions but insists on their philosophical consistency, separating emotion from philosophical rigor.
- This discussion centers on biblical inerrancy rather than core Christian doctrines, suggesting its doctrinal separability if the stories are considered allegorical or inaccurate.
